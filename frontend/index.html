Module 0 — Kickoff

15 min  |  The big picture. See the whole loop before you build any piece of it.

The Physical AI pipeline

Every robot that does something useful follows the same loop: look, decide, act, repeat. That's not a simplification — that really is the whole thing.

Today you'll build each piece. By Module 4 your hand will be controlling a simulated robot arm in real time. The same loop, the same code pattern, runs inside every robot from surgical arms to self-driving cars.

[Webcam] → [Perception Layer] → [State Vector] → [Sim Agent] → [Action] ↑ ↓ [World/Sim] ←───────────────────────────────────────────────── [Effect] ↑ [Foundation Model Reasoning]

Notice the arrows go all the way around. That's the point — actions feed back into what the robot sees next. It's not a one-shot prediction. It's a continuous conversation between the robot and the world.

The state vector

At every step, the robot's world gets squashed down into a list of numbers — one array that captures everything it needs to decide what to do next. That's the state vector. When your hand is open: big numbers. Fist: small numbers. Robot gripper: knows exactly how wide to open. That's the whole idea.

Module 1 — Perception

How perception works
What is MediaPipe?
EveryoneMediaPipe is Google's library for detecting human body parts — hands, face, pose — in camera frames in real time. It's the 'eyes' of our robot: without it we'd see raw pixels, not hand positions.
Goes deeperOfficial Hand Landmarker docs — what the model detects, how it runs, configuration options.
Goes deepestMediaPipe Hands paper (2020) — how the 21-keypoint model was designed and trained on 30K real images + synthetic data.
Why 21 landmarks? Why normalised coordinates?
EveryoneThe model detects 21 specific points — one per knuckle, fingertip, and wrist. Returned as fractions of the frame (0–1) so the same code works at any camera resolution.
Goes deeperSee the MediaPipe model page — 21 hand-knuckle coordinates trained on 30K real images.
Goes deepestWhy normalise? A robot arm doesn't care about screen pixels — it cares about proportional positions. This is the same reason robot joint encoders output normalised readings.

Your camera is running 30 frames per second. Each frame, MediaPipe finds your hand and marks 21 specific points on it — fingertips, knuckles, wrist. We then measure the angle at three of those knuckles. That angle, in degrees, becomes a number your robot can use.

Nothing runs on the server for this module — it's all inside your browser tab. Start the camera below and watch the state vector update live as you move your hand.

Landmarks used for joint angles:

θ_index lm0 → lm5 → lm6
θ_middle lm0 → lm9 → lm10
θ_ring (exercise) lm0 → lm13 → lm14
θ_thumb lm0 → lm1 → lm2
lm8 tip used in Module 4
Angle formula
θ = atan2(|u × v|, u · v)

u = lm_base − lm_vertex, v = lm_tip − lm_vertex. Returns the opening angle at the MCP joint in degrees (0° = closed, ~180° = fully open).

What is a simulation environment?
What is Gymnasium?
EveryoneGymnasium wraps physics simulations behind a standard interface. Think of it as a flight simulator for robots — safe to crash, reset, and try again infinitely, without touching hardware.
Goes deepergymnasium.farama.org — official docs, all environments, API reference.
Goes deepestWhy simulation? Real robots break, cost thousands, and can't run 50,000 experiments overnight. Policies trained in simulation are then deployed on hardware (sim-to-real transfer) — used by Boston Dynamics, NVIDIA Isaac Lab, and every major robot lab.
What is MuJoCo?
EveryoneMuJoCo (Multi-Joint dynamics with Contact) is Google DeepMind's physics engine. It simulates forces, collisions, and inertia of robot joints accurately and fast.
Goes deepermujoco.org — official site. Docs overview.
Goes deepestDeepMind blog: Opening up MuJoCo — why it was open-sourced in 2021.
What is the Reacher robot?
EveryoneReacher-v5 is a simulated two-link arm (shoulder + elbow) that must reach a target. The simplest environment that captures the core challenge of robot arm control: moving precisely against inertia.
Goes deeperGymnasium Reacher-v5 docs — full observation/action space specification.
Goes deepestTwo-link arms appear in SCARA assembly robots, the da Vinci surgical wrist, and warehouse pickers. The same torque-based control you're doing here — with a trained policy instead of your hand — is exactly how real arms operate.

You can't crash a simulation. You can't break a simulated robot. That's the whole point. Gymnasium is the standard way Python code talks to physics environments — you call env.step(action), get back observations and a reward, and loop. Same interface whether it's a cartoon pole or a humanoid.

Observation space — what the agent sees

Ten numbers. That's all the arm knows about its own existence. Here's what each one means:

  • obs[0,2] — cos/sin of shoulder joint angle
  • obs[1,3] — cos/sin of elbow joint angle
  • obs[4,5] — target x, y position
  • obs[6,7] — joint angular velocities ω₁, ω₂
  • obs[8,9] — fingertip−target vector (distance = norm(obs[8:10]))

These are the live values from the running arm — same panel on the right side of the simulation stream below. On a real arm, they'd come from joint encoders and inertial sensors. Here they come from physics equations. Same format, same meaning.

Action space — what the agent can do

Two numbers, both between −1 and 1. Each one is a torque — how hard to push a joint, and in which direction. Drag the sliders below and watch the arm react.

Notice it doesn't snap to a position. You're applying force, not setting an angle. The arm overshoots, corrects, oscillates. That's real physics — real robot arms do the same thing.

Why cos/sin instead of the raw angle? Angles wrap: 359° and 1° are 358 units apart numerically but one degree apart physically. A network trained on raw angles would freak out at that discontinuity — it'd see the joint at 359° and think it needs a massive correction to reach 1°. That correction could physically break a real servo. cos/sin never wraps. Same value at every rotation.

Module 3 — Reinforcement Learning

30 min  |  Watch a robot learn from scratch. It's bad at first. Then suddenly it's not.

What is reinforcement learning?
What is Reinforcement Learning? What is an MDP?
EveryoneRL is how robots learn by trial and error — no labelled examples, just a reward signal. An MDP (Markov Decision Process) formalises this: state → agent → action → reward → new state → repeat.
Goes deeperHuggingFace Deep RL Course — Unit 1: the clearest free introduction to RL and MDPs, with visual examples.
Goes deepestWhy RL for robots? Hand-coding rules for every robot situation is impossible. RL discovers strategies automatically through millions of simulated attempts — the same approach used by Boston Dynamics for Spot's locomotion.
What is PPO?
EveryonePPO (Proximal Policy Optimisation) is the RL algorithm we use. It learns a neural network policy by updating it in small, safe steps — preventing catastrophic policy collapses.
Goes deeperSB3 PPO docs. Original PPO paper (Schulman et al., 2017).
Goes deepestPPO is the most widely deployed deep RL algorithm in physical robotics. OpenAI used it for Dactyl, NVIDIA Isaac Lab uses it by default, DeepMind used variants for locomotion.

The agent tries something. Gets a reward. Tries again. That's all. No dataset, no labels, no supervisor — just try-fail-adjust, millions of times. It's how you learn to ride a bike, minus the scraped knees. And it's the only way anyone's figured out how to get a robot arm to handle objects it's never seen before.

The MDP — Markov Decision Process

Every RL problem can be written as a 4-tuple (S, A, R, γ):

  • S — State space: everything the agent can observe (e.g. the 4 CartPole values)
  • A — Action space: everything the agent can do (e.g. push left or push right)
  • R — Reward function: +1 every timestep the pole stays upright; 0 on failure
  • γ — Discount factor: how much the agent values future rewards vs. immediate ones (0 = greedy, 1 = patient)

The "Markov" property means the current state contains all the information needed — no memory of previous states required.

Policy, value function, and PPO

The policy is the agent's brain — a neural network that takes in sensor readings and outputs motor commands. On a real robot this runs at 50–100 Hz without stopping. What we're training here is a tiny version of exactly that.

PPO is how we train it. The clever part: it makes the policy slightly better each step without accidentally making it catastrophically worse. Other algorithms could learn faster but blow up. PPO is the safe option. It's what Boston Dynamics, NVIDIA Isaac Lab, and most serious robot labs use today.

Watch for the inflection. The reward is flat for a while — that's the agent fumbling around randomly. Then it suddenly jumps. That's the moment it figured out the balancing trick. Everything to the left of that jump would have been expensive hardware time on a real robot. That's why we simulate first.

PPO CartPole-v1 — live training

50,000 timesteps. The chart updates as it trains. Max reward is 500 — a good agent holds the pole up indefinitely and gets 500 every episode.

On a GPU cluster this would finish in under a minute. On your laptop it'll take a few. That's still vastly cheaper than the equivalent real-robot time — which is why everyone trains in simulation first.

What is CartPole and why do we train on it?
EveryoneCartPole is a physics simulation where a pole balances on a cart. Push left or right to keep it upright. It's the 'Hello World' of RL — simple enough to train in minutes, yet it demonstrates the full reward-driven loop used in real robots.
Goes deeperGymnasium CartPole-v1 docs — observation space, reward, episode termination.
Goes deepestWhy not train on Reacher? CartPole converges in ~50K steps; Reacher needs millions. For a 30-minute session, CartPole lets you see the full learning curve in real time. That curve shape is identical in production robot training.

In a sim-to-real deployment, 50,000 simulated steps might take seconds on a GPU — but represent months of real robot time. This is why simulation-first training is the standard approach in Physical AI.

Closing the loop
What is teleoperation?
EveryoneTeleoperation: a human directly controls a robot's actuators through a sensory interface — you move your hand, the robot arm moves. You ARE the controller.
Goes deeperExamples: the da Vinci surgical robot, NASA Mars rovers, warehouse picking robots. In modern Physical AI, teleoperation is also used to collect demonstration data for training imitation-learning policies.
Goes deepestProduction data collection rigs: Mobile ALOHA, UMI, ALOHA 2. What you're building in Module 4 is the same pattern at minimal scale.

This is where it all connects. Your hand. A physics simulation. A live robot arm reacting to your movement. No AI, no training — just the simplest possible mapping: finger right means torque right. That's the whole controller.

This is also how surgeons control robotic scalpels, how warehouse operators control picking arms from a safe room, and how the first Apollo lunar rover was driven from Earth. Teleoperation is older than AI and still everywhere.

Landmark → action mapping

MediaPipe returns landmark 8 (index fingertip) as normalised coordinates in [0, 1]. The Reacher action space expects torques in [−1, 1]. The mapping is a simple linear stretch:

action = 2 × landmark_x − 1

Move your finger from left to right: torque goes from −1 to +1. Up/down maps to the second joint. That's it — the entire "brain" in this module is two lines of maths.

Teleoperation and imitation learning

What you're doing here is teleoperation — a human directly controls a robot's actuators through a sensory interface. This is how large demonstration datasets are collected for imitation learning algorithms like Behaviour Cloning (BC), ACT, and Diffusion Policy.

The key insight: the same physical setup — camera + landmarks + action mapping — that you're using interactively today is used in real robotics labs to collect thousands of demonstrations that train production robot policies.

You're pushing, not placing. There's no "go to this angle" — you're applying force, and the arm goes where physics takes it. Hold the slider at +1 and watch it spin forever. That's inertia. To stop somewhere specific you have to push backwards before you arrive. Same problem every real robot servo faces. RL (Module 3) learns to handle this automatically.

Module 5 — Foundation Models

25 min  |  Replace the two-line "brain" with a billion-parameter one. Same pipeline, different controller.

Vision-Language Models for robotics
What is a VLM? What is a VLA?
EveryoneA Vision-Language Model (VLM) accepts images AND text as input and outputs text. A Vision-Language-Action model (VLA) goes further: it outputs robot actions directly — no separate perception or planning step.
Goes deeperAccessible overview: Google DeepMind's RT-2 announcement — the first production VLA.
Goes deepestProduction VLAs: RT-2 (2023), OpenVLA (2024), π0 (2024). What you're building in Module 5 is a minimal version of the same architecture.

Gemini has seen most of the internet. It knows what objects look like, what "to the left" means, what a hand reaching for something looks like. We're going to exploit that. Send it a camera frame, ask it what the robot should do, and it will answer — without a single line of robot-specific training.

The catch: it can only answer in text. Our job is to write a prompt that forces the text into a format we can parse and act on. That prompt — two lines of JSON schema — is the entire interface between a billion-parameter model and a physical machine.

The Vision-Language-Action (VLA) pattern
  • Capture: grab one camera frame as a JPEG
  • Encode: convert to base64 — the format the API accepts
  • Prompt: send frame + text instruction to the model
  • Parse: extract structured JSON from the response
  • Act: map the JSON action field to robot commands

This is exactly the pattern used in production VLA systems like RT-2, OpenVLA, and π0 — just at much larger scale and with fine-tuned models.

Why prompting matters

The model has never seen your robot. It doesn't know what "joint 1" means. The prompt is how you tell it. Change the prompt, change the vocabulary — the robot learns a new language without retraining. That's the power, and the fragility, of prompt-based control.

Sim-to-real transfer

The CartPole agent you trained works in simulation. Getting it to work on a real cart-pole is harder — friction is different, sensors have noise, the physics don't quite match. That gap is called the sim-to-real gap. The main fix: train across randomised physics (different masses, friction values, noise levels) so the policy doesn't memorise one specific simulator.

That's domain randomisation. It's why NVIDIA Isaac Lab trains with thousands of randomised parallel environments. And it's why the policies that come out the other end actually work on real hardware.

What to explore next

  • Isaac Lab — NVIDIA's GPU-accelerated robot learning framework built on Isaac Sim; runs thousands of parallel MuJoCo-quality environments for fast sim-to-real transfer.
  • LeRobot — HuggingFace's end-to-end imitation learning library; the natural next step after Module 4's teleoperation, turning your demonstration data into a trained Diffusion Policy or ACT policy.
  • ROS 2 — the Robot Operating System; the industry-standard middleware for connecting perception, planning, and actuation nodes on real hardware across a distributed network.

Webcam detects shoulder/neck landmarks. Agent adapts alert timing from your observed correction history — not a fixed threshold.		 Laptop Beginner Remove the adaptation loop and it's a static classifier with a buzzer. The feedback is the product. M1 · M3
2 Sim-to-Sim Transfer
Train PPO on CartPole. Deploy into a modified env (heavier pole, wind noise). Measure degradation. Apply domain randomisation and retrain.		 Laptop Intermediate This is the sim-to-real problem without real hardware. Policy degradation across physics regimes has no analogue in static ML. M2 · M3
3 Behaviour Cloning Game Agent
Use hand control from Module 4 to play a simple Pygame environment. Record (obs, action) pairs. Train an MLP clone. Deploy and observe where it fails.		 Laptop Intermediate The Physical AI part is the clone — covariate shift is concretely observable when the agent encounters states outside the demo distribution. This is the ACT/Diffusion Policy pattern at minimal scale. M1 · M4 · M5
4 Sign Language Recogniser with Corrective Loop
Real-time letter recognition from hand landmarks. Model confidence feeds back to prompt the user to re-sign ambiguous letters.		 Laptop Beginner–Intermediate The agent's uncertainty actively shapes the human's behaviour. Remove the confidence feedback and you have a static recogniser. M1 · M5
5 PID Tuner via RL
Simulated pendulum with a PID controller. Use RL to learn the gain parameters by treating them as the action space. Compare learned vs hand-tuned gains.		 Laptop Intermediate RL solving a physical control problem — the same challenge engineers face deploying on hardware with unknown friction and inertia. M2 · M3
6 Multi-Step VLA Planner
Give Gemini a verbal instruction ("move to the red zone"). It decomposes it into sub-goals. Each sub-goal executes via the Module 4 pipeline. Chain three or more steps.		 Laptop Intermediate This is the SayCan / RT-2 architecture at minimal scale. The brittleness of VLM output under real conditions is the lesson — and the open research problem. M4 · M5
7 Gaze-Tracked Servo Turret
Pi Camera + MediaPipe detects a face or hand. Two SG90 servos pan/tilt to track it. Apply the Module 4 linear mapping (2x−1) directly to servo angle.		 Pi + 2× servo Intermediate The turret loses its target within seconds without the loop — it is physically broken open-loop. First project where real sensor noise and servo inertia are tangible constraints. M1 · M4
8 Imitation Learning Arm
2-servo arm on Pi. Record (landmark state, servo angle) demonstrations manually. Train a small MLP. Deploy. Document where it drifts.		 Pi + 2× servo Intermediate–Advanced Behaviour Cloning on real hardware. Covariate shift is physically visible — the arm drifts into states it never saw during demo and the policy breaks. The failure mode is the educational content. M1 · M4
9 RL Balancer
Train PPO on a MuJoCo pendulum. Deploy onto a physical pendulum with Pi + servo + encoder or camera. Measure and document the sim-to-real gap quantitatively. 4–6 week project.		 Pi + servo + sensor Advanced The policy only exists because of millions of simulated feedback loops. The sim-to-real gap — dramatic due to friction and encoder noise — is what labs like Tedrake's at MIT study at scale. M2 · M3
How to start: pick one project from your hardware tier. Get perception working first — before wiring up the action. The moment the loop closes and you see feedback in real time is the same moment as Module 4. That's the point.
